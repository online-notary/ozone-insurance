Quick start

  1. Enter the purchase price, down payment, loan rate and loan length under Purchase & loan and Financing.
  2. Enter the yearly rent and costs (property tax, maintenance, insurance and the others).
  3. Check the growth assumptions: appreciation, tax rates and selling costs.
  4. Read the Summary tab. It shows what you would earn if you sold at the end of each year, and which year looks best.

Every result updates as you type. The example numbers on the page are only a starting point; replace them with your own.

New purchase or in-force

New purchase projects every year from the day you buy. Use it to test a property you are thinking about buying.

In-force is for a property you already own. Enter the year you bought it and the year your real numbers run through (usually this year). An Actuals tab appears where you type what really happened each year. Years after that are projected. The summary then answers one question: should you sell now or keep holding, and until when?

How periods work

Most inputs can change over time. Each line is a period: a From year, a To year, and an amount.

  • Basis sets what the amount means: a flat dollar amount per year, or a percentage of the purchase price, the property's current value, the rent, or the selling price.
  • Grows is a yearly increase within that period. Rent of $42,000 growing 3% becomes $43,260 the next year.
  • + Add period starts a new period the year after the last one ends. Use it for changes such as a rate reset or a rent increase in a later year.
  • Periods always run back to back. Only the first From year can be typed; each later one starts automatically the year after the previous period ends. To leave a cost out for some years, add a period with an amount of 0.

The inputs

Total cost of purchase and down payment
The total cost includes the price plus closing costs, loan fees and any repairs you make before renting. The down payment is the cash you put in; the rest is the loan.
Loan duration and interest rate
The mortgage. The payment is split into interest and principal every year. If the rate changes, the payment is recalculated for the remaining term.
Shortfalls and the shortfall loan rate
When rent does not cover all costs in a year, the gap is added to a separate shortfall loan. It grows at the shortfall loan rate and is repaid from the sale. If you pay shortfalls from your own pocket, set this rate to what that cash could earn elsewhere (your hurdle rate), not 0%.
Rental income
Gross yearly rent before any costs.
Property tax, maintenance, other costs
Yearly operating costs. The three other costs can be renamed (for example insurance, vacancy, management).
Appreciation rate
How fast the property's value grows each year.
Depreciation
The yearly tax deduction for the building's wear and tear. Residential property is usually depreciated over 27.5 years. It lowers your income tax now, but part of it is taxed back when you sell (recapture).
Advanced inputs (taxes)
Income tax rate, depreciation and tax losses affect every year you own the property. Capital gains and depreciation recapture rates only matter when you sell. The defaults fit many middle-income families; tap the i next to each one for a plain explanation.
Selling price and selling costs
By default you sell at 100% of market value and pay 6% in selling costs. Lower the percentage to test selling below market.
Planned sale year and hurdle rate
The year you expect to sell, and the return you could earn elsewhere. The hurdle rate is used to compare holding against selling.

Entering actual numbers (in-force)

  • Each row of the Actuals tab is one past year. Grey numbers are what the assumptions would give; leave a cell blank to keep them.
  • At a minimum, enter this year's market value and loan balance. Those two numbers drive the sell-now figure.
  • You can copy a block of cells from Excel or Google Sheets, click the first cell in the table, and paste.
  • If you refinanced and took cash out, enter the new, higher loan balance for that year. The extra cash shows up as money to you.
  • After the actual years: "Continue from last actual" starts projections from your real numbers for the last actual year. "Use assumptions as entered" ignores them for future years.

Reading the results

  • Summary: the key numbers, a chart of your return if sold at the end of each year, and total profit by year. The gold dot and gold cards mark the best year.
  • Hold or sell? (in-force only): compares selling now with holding to each future year, and tells you which comes out ahead at your hurdle rate.
  • Annual ledger: every number, year by year: income, costs, mortgage, taxes, cash flow, loan balance, equity and the result if sold that year. Rows marked A use actual numbers.
  • Cost detail: each cost by year, with totals.

Numbers in parentheses, like ($2,028), are money going out.

Saving and comparing scenarios

On the Scenarios tab, name and save the current inputs. Tick Compare to draw a saved scenario's return line on the Summary chart. Scenarios are kept in this browser only. Use Copy to keep a scenario as text, and Import to load it again later or on another computer.

Terms

NOI (net operating income)
Rent minus operating costs, before the mortgage and income tax.
Cap rate
NOI divided by the purchase price. A quick measure of the property's earning power without a loan.
Cash-on-cash
Yearly cash flow before tax divided by the cash you invested.
IRR (internal rate of return)
Your yearly return on the money you put in, counting every year's cash flow and the sale. It is the single best number for comparing with other investments.
IRR from today
In-force only. Treats the money you would get by selling now as your investment, and measures what keeping it in the property earns from here.
NPV (net present value)
How many dollars better off you are compared with earning your hurdle rate elsewhere. Positive means holding beats the alternative.
Equity
Property value minus everything you owe on it.
Equity multiple
Total cash you get back divided by the cash you put in. 2.0× means you doubled your money.
Net proceeds if sold
What you keep after selling costs, paying off the loans, and taxes on the sale.
