Rental property guide

How to Analyze a Rental Property

A practical guide to judging a rental, deciding when to sell, and comparing it fairly with the other investments you are considering.

Madhu GowdaFinancial Professional, Ozone Insurance Services
Open the Rental Property Illustrator
Contents

    Why this guide exists

    Most rental property decisions are made without the one number every other investment is judged by: the annual return on the money you put in. Owners usually know the purchase price, the rent and today's value. Very few know their internal rate of return, what they would actually walk away with after taxes if they sold, or whether keeping the property beats moving that money somewhere else.

    Insurance and investment products come with an illustration that shows year by year how they are expected to perform. This guide does the same for a rental. It explains how to build a year-by-year illustration, how to read the numbers that matter, how to decide when to sell, and how to compare a rental fairly with the other investments you are considering.

    The examples use the Rental Property Illustrator, but the method works with any spreadsheet.

    The four questions every analysis must answer

    A rental is a good investment only if it answers all four of these well. Rising value alone answers none of them.

    1. Does it pay for itself each year? Rent minus every cost, including the mortgage, is your yearly cash flow. A property that needs money from you every month is a bet on future appreciation.
    2. What is my return on the cash I put in? Measured as an annual rate (IRR), after taxes, counting every year of cash flow and the eventual sale. This is the number you compare with other investments.
    3. What would I keep if I sold? The sale price minus selling costs, the loan payoff, depreciation recapture and capital gains tax. This is your real equity, and it is usually well below "value minus mortgage."
    4. Is keeping it better than moving the money? Compared year by year with what the same money would earn elsewhere (your hurdle rate). This tells you when to sell.

    Step by step: building an illustration

    An honest illustration uses realistic, slightly conservative assumptions. The goal is not a pretty number; it is a number you would still believe if things went a little worse than hoped.

    For a property you are considering (New purchase)

    1. Purchase and loan. Enter the total cost of purchase (the price plus closing costs, typically 2–3% more), the down payment, loan length and interest rate. Use the rate you would actually get today, not last year's.
    2. Rent. Use rents for comparable homes nearby, not the seller's estimate. Set a yearly increase of 2–3% unless your market clearly supports more.
    3. Every cost. Property tax, insurance, maintenance (1% of value per year is a common rule), HOA, vacancy (5–8% of rent) and property management (8–10% of rent) even if you plan to self-manage. Your time has value, and a future buyer will price it in.
    4. Growth and taxes. Appreciation of 3–4% per year is a reasonable long-run planning figure for many markets. Check the tax rates under Advanced inputs: income tax, capital gains and depreciation recapture. The defaults (27%, 20% and 27%) fit many middle-income families; adjust them to your situation.
    5. Selling. Selling costs of 5–7% of the price cover commissions, transfer taxes and closing.
    6. Your comparison point. Set the hurdle rate to what the down payment would realistically earn elsewhere (see the NPV section).

    For a property you already own (In-force)

    1. Set Illustration type to In-force. Enter the year you bought it and the year your actual numbers run through.
    2. Enter the original purchase price, down payment and loan, as they were at purchase.
    3. On the Actuals tab, enter what really happened each year: rent, taxes, costs, loan balance and, at a minimum, today's market value and loan balance. Old tax returns (Schedule E) and loan statements have most of these figures.
    4. Set the assumptions for the future years, then read the Hold or sell? panel.

    Use periods for anything that changes

    Every input can change over time. Add a period when a rate resets, rent jumps after a renovation, or a cost starts or stops. Periods run back to back with no gaps; a cost that should disappear for a few years gets a period with an amount of 0.

    The key measures, with a worked example

    Each measure answers a different question, and no single one is enough. The examples use the illustrator's sample property: a home with a total cost of $450,000, bought with 25% down, a 30-year loan at 6.75%, and $42,000 a year in rent.

    Measure What it tells you Sample property What good looks like
    Cash invested The down payment (closing costs are part of the total cost) $112,500 Starting point for every return
    NOI (net operating income) Rent minus operating costs, before the mortgage $24,240 in year 1 Positive and growing
    Cap rate NOI ÷ purchase price: earning power without a loan 5.39% Compare with today's mortgage rate
    Cash-on-cash Pre-tax cash flow ÷ cash invested –1.80% in year 1 Positive from year 1 or 2
    IRR if sold in year 10 Annual after-tax return on your cash, including the sale 10.3% Above your hurdle rate
    Equity multiple Total cash back ÷ cash in 2.64× by year 10 Read alongside the years it took
    NPV at 7% Dollars gained over earning 7% elsewhere Highest at year 30: $98,097 Positive

    Cap rate versus your mortgage rate

    When the cap rate is below the mortgage rate (5.39% versus 6.75% here), each borrowed dollar costs more than it earns. That is called negative leverage, and it is why the sample property loses about $2,000 in its first year. The deal can still work, but only through rent growth and appreciation. You should see that clearly before you buy, not discover it later.

    IRR: the number that compares with everything else

    IRR is the single annual rate that makes all your cash flows, in and out, add up to zero. It accounts for timing: a dollar back in year 2 counts more than a dollar back in year 20. It is the same kind of number as a fund's annualized return or an IUL illustration's internal rate of return, which is what makes fair comparisons possible.

    0 = −Cash invested + Σt=1..n Cash flowt ÷ (1 + IRR)t + Net sale proceedsn ÷ (1 + IRR)n

    Equity multiple: simple, but blind to time

    A 2.5× multiple is excellent over 8 years and weak over 30. Always read it with the holding period next to it.

    Net proceeds: your real equity

    "Value minus mortgage" overstates what you own. Net proceeds subtract selling costs, depreciation recapture and capital gains tax as well. On a long-held property the gap can easily be $50,000–$100,000 or more.

    Taxes: where the real return is won or lost

    Taxes can move a rental's after-tax return by several percentage points, so an illustration that ignores them is not comparable with anything. Four rules do most of the work. Confirm how each applies to you with a tax professional.

    Depreciation

    The IRS lets you deduct the building's cost (not the land) over 27.5 years for residential property. On a $450,000 home with an 80% building share, that is about $13,090 a year. It is a paper expense: no cash leaves your pocket, but taxable income falls. That is why many rentals show a tax loss while putting cash in the owner's pocket.

    Passive losses: carry forward or offset

    Whether that tax loss saves you money now depends on your income and how involved you are.

    Your situation Typical treatment Setting in the illustrator
    Actively manage the rental, income under $100,000 Up to $25,000 a year of losses can offset wages Offset other income each year
    Income between $100,000 and $150,000 The $25,000 allowance phases out Usually carry forward
    Income above $150,000, not a real estate professional Losses are suspended and carried forward Carry forward
    Qualify as a real estate professional Losses can offset other income Offset other income each year

    Carried-forward losses are not lost. They offset future rental profits, and whatever remains is released when you sell. The difference is timing, and timing changes IRR.

    Depreciation recapture

    When you sell, the depreciation you took is taxed at up to 25% federally. You cannot avoid it by not claiming depreciation, because the IRS treats it as "allowed or allowable." A long-held property can carry a recapture bill in the tens of thousands of dollars.

    Capital gains

    The rest of your gain is taxed at long-term capital gains rates (0%, 15% or 20% federally, plus the 3.8% net investment income tax for higher earners, plus state tax; California taxes gains as ordinary income). A 1031 exchange can defer both recapture and capital gains if you buy another investment property, but it keeps your money in real estate.

    Finding the best time to sell

    The best year to sell depends on which question you ask, and IRR and NPV ask different ones. In the sample property, IRR flattens out after about year 9, while NPV keeps growing for all 30 years.

    • IRR asks how fast your money is growing. It peaks once the early, loss-making years are behind you, then drifts down as more and more equity sits inside the property earning a lower rate.
    • NPV asks how much wealth you end up with compared with your alternative. As long as the property earns more than the hurdle rate on the equity inside it, holding longer adds dollars, even while IRR is flat or falling.
    • When they disagree, NPV is usually the better guide to total wealth. IRR is the better tool for comparing with other investments of a similar length.

    Hold or sell: looking forward from today

    For a property you already own, the past is fixed. The question is whether to keep today's equity in the house or move it. The illustrator treats your net proceeds if you sold today as the investment, then measures what holding earns from here ("IRR from today").

    • If the IRR from today is above your hurdle rate, and NPV is positive, holding is paying you.
    • If it is below the hurdle, your equity has outgrown the property. Selling and redeploying the money (or a 1031 exchange, or a cash-out refinance) may serve you better.

    Many long-held rentals fall into the second group: a large amount of equity earning a modest rent, with a growing recapture and capital gains bill.

    Comparing a rental with other investments

    A fair comparison puts every option on the same footing: after-tax IRR, over the same number of years, on the same dollars. Then it weighs what the return alone does not show: risk, liquidity, effort and concentration.

    Six rules for an apples-to-apples comparison

    1. After tax against after tax. A 9% pre-tax return in a taxable account is not the same as 9% from a tax-advantaged product. Compare what you keep.
    2. Same holding period. Compare a 10-year rental with 10 years of the alternative. Short holds punish rentals because buying and selling costs 8–10% of the price.
    3. Same dollars. Count everything you put into the rental: down payment, closing costs, repairs and any monthly shortfalls you covered. Then ask what that same money would have done elsewhere.
    4. Net of exit costs. Use the rental's net proceeds after selling costs and taxes, and the alternative's value after any surrender charges, fees or taxes on withdrawal.
    5. Leverage changes the risk. A rental's IRR is boosted by the mortgage, and so is its downside. Compare the cap rate (the unleveraged return) with unleveraged alternatives, and the leveraged IRR with alternatives of similar risk.
    6. Include the hidden costs. Your time, vacancies, a bad tenant, a $15,000 roof. If you would not do the work for free, charge the property for it in the illustration.

    Putting it into practice

    1. Get the rental's IRR from the illustrator for the holding period you are considering, using realistic assumptions.
    2. Get the alternative's IRR for the same period. For insurance or annuity products, use the illustrated IRR on cash value and look at both the guaranteed and the non-guaranteed columns.
    3. Put both on an after-tax basis.
    4. Set the hurdle rate in the illustrator to the alternative's after-tax IRR. The NPV then shows, in dollars, how much better or worse the rental does than that alternative.
    5. Weigh the gap against the trade-offs. If the rental wins by 3 percentage points a year, decide whether that pays you for the leverage, the work, the lack of liquidity and having a large share of your net worth in one building.

    Example. The sample property earns about 10.3% a year over 10 years on $112,500. A portfolio earning 6% after tax would grow the same $112,500 to roughly $201,000. The rental's cash flows plus its net sale proceeds come to about $297,000 (2.64×). The rental is ahead by roughly $96,000 before accounting for time, risk and concentration. That is the number to weigh, not the headline price gain.

    Diversification still matters

    One rental can easily be 40–60% of a family's net worth, tied to one city, one employer base and one roof. Even a good deal can make a portfolio fragile. The right question is often not "rental or not" but "how much of my wealth should sit in this one asset?"

    Common mistakes

    • Counting only appreciation. "I bought for $400,000 and it is worth $700,000" ignores the down payment, every shortfall you covered, selling costs and taxes. The IRR is often far lower than the price gain suggests.
    • Forgetting the money you added. Monthly shortfalls, a new roof, a vacancy: all of it is money invested and belongs in the return.
    • Leaving out vacancy and management. Even self-managed rentals have empty months and your time has a cost.
    • Ignoring taxes at sale. Depreciation recapture and capital gains can take a large share of the gain.
    • Assuming the past will repeat. Ten years of fast appreciation and low rates do not set the assumptions for the next ten.
    • Comparing pre-tax rental numbers with after-tax alternatives, or a 30-year rental with a 5-year alternative.
    • Holding by default. Not deciding is a decision to keep your equity where it is. Re-run the hold-or-sell test every year or two.

    Checklist

    Before you buy

    • Rent based on comparable homes, not the seller's figures
    • Every cost included: taxes, insurance, maintenance, HOA, vacancy, management
    • Today's loan rate, and the cap rate compared with it
    • Cash flow in years 1–3: can you carry a shortfall comfortably?
    • IRR at 5, 10 and 15 years, after tax
    • IRR compared with your realistic alternative, after tax
    • A downside case: 0–1% appreciation, a vacant year, a 15% repair bill

    If you already own a rental

    • Actual numbers entered from tax returns and loan statements
    • Today's market value and loan balance
    • Net proceeds if sold today, after recapture and capital gains
    • IRR so far, and IRR from today for each future year
    • Hurdle rate set to what the equity could earn elsewhere
    • Options reviewed: hold, sell, 1031 exchange, cash-out refinance
    • How much of your net worth sits in this one property

    Depreciation recapture Tax at sale (up to 25% federally) on the depreciation you deducted
